It usually starts small. Your mother asks the same question twice in one phone call. Your father, who balanced the checkbook for fifty years, leaves bills unpaid. Then one day the stove is left on, or a neighbor calls because Dad was found walking down the road in his slippers, and the worry you have been pushing aside becomes impossible to ignore.

If you are caring for a parent with dementia, you are far from alone. The Alzheimer's Association estimates that more than 188,000 Georgians are living with Alzheimer's disease, supported by roughly 391,000 unpaid family caregivers. Most of those families share the same goal: keep their loved one at home, safe and as comfortable as possible, for as long as they can.

This guide covers what to expect as dementia progresses, practical ways to make daily life easier, how to protect your parent's safety, and how Georgia's [Elderly and Disabled Waiver Program (EDWP)](/programs/edwp) can bring trained help into the home.

## Understanding What Dementia Changes

Dementia is not one disease. It is a group of symptoms caused by conditions that damage the brain, with Alzheimer's disease being the most common. Each type follows its own path, but most families notice changes in a few areas:

- **Memory:** forgetting recent conversations, appointments, or whether a meal was eaten
- **Judgment:** trouble with money, driving, or recognizing a scam
- **Language:** searching for words or losing the thread of a conversation
- **Mood and behavior:** anxiety, irritability, suspicion, or restlessness, especially late in the day
- **Daily tasks:** difficulty with bathing, dressing, cooking, or taking medications correctly

> Dementia takes memories, but it does not take away a person's need for dignity, comfort, and connection. Good care starts with meeting your parent where they are today.

If you have not yet had a formal evaluation, ask your parent's doctor for one. Some causes of memory problems, such as thyroid issues, infections, or medication side effects, can be treated, and a clear diagnosis helps you plan.

## Building a Calm, Predictable Day

For a person with dementia, the world can feel confusing and fast. Routine is one of the most powerful tools a family has because it reduces decisions and surprises.

### Keep a steady rhythm

Try to wake up, eat, bathe, and go to bed at about the same times each day. Plan harder tasks, such as bathing or doctor visits, for the time of day when your parent is usually at their best. For many people that is the morning.

### Simplify choices and steps

Instead of "What do you want to wear?", offer two outfits. Break tasks into single steps: "Let's put your arm in this sleeve" works better than "Get dressed." Lay out clothing in the order it goes on.

### Communicate with patience

- Speak slowly, in short sentences, and make eye contact
- Ask one question at a time and allow extra time to answer
- Avoid arguing or correcting. If your mother asks for her own mother, who passed long ago, respond to the feeling behind the question ("You're thinking about her. Tell me about her.")
- Use a warm tone and gentle touch; people with dementia often read emotion long after words become hard

### Watch for late-day restlessness

Many people with dementia become more agitated or confused in the late afternoon and evening, sometimes called "sundowning." Turning on lights before dusk, limiting caffeine and naps late in the day, playing familiar music, and keeping evenings quiet can help.

## Making the Home Safer

As judgment and memory change, the home itself needs to change too. A few adjustments can prevent the most common emergencies:

1. **Reduce fall risks.** Remove loose rugs, add night lights in hallways and bathrooms, and install grab bars near the toilet and shower.
2. **Secure dangerous items.** Lock up medications, cleaning products, firearms, and car keys. Consider stove knob covers or an automatic shut-off device.
3. **Plan for wandering.** Wandering is common and can happen even in early stages. Door chimes or alarms, a medical ID bracelet, and a recent photo on hand for emergencies all help. Let trusted neighbors know.
4. **Manage medications carefully.** Use a pill organizer or locked dispenser, and make sure someone supervises doses as memory declines.
5. **Keep important papers in order.** If your parent can still participate, talk with an attorney about a durable power of attorney and a health care directive while they can share their wishes.

## How EDWP Can Help Georgia Families

Caring for a parent with dementia around the clock is not something one person can sustain alone. Georgia's **Elderly and Disabled Waiver Program (EDWP)**, delivered through **CCSP** and **SOURCE**, is a Medicaid program designed to help older adults and adults with disabilities stay at home instead of moving to a nursing facility.

EDWP does not qualify someone based on a dementia diagnosis alone. Your parent generally needs to be **65 or older** (or an adult 21 or older with a qualifying physical disability), be eligible for **Georgia Medicaid**, and have care needs serious enough to meet the state's **nursing facility level of care**. Many people in the middle and later stages of dementia meet that standard because they need hands-on help or supervision every day.

For families living with dementia, several EDWP services are especially valuable:

- **Personal Support Services (PSS):** a trained aide helps with bathing, dressing, grooming, meals, light housekeeping, and keeping the day on track
- **Adult Day Health:** a supervised daytime program with health monitoring, meals, and social activities, giving your parent stimulation and giving you time to work or rest
- **Respite care:** planned breaks so family caregivers can recharge before burnout sets in
- **Structured Family Caregiver (SFC):** training, coaching, and a stipend for a family member who serves as the primary caregiver
- **Emergency Response Services:** a 24-hour alert system so help is a button press away
- **Home-delivered meals:** nutritious meals for parents who can no longer cook safely

You can read more about how these programs work in our guide to [EDWP, CCSP, and SOURCE](/blog/edwp-ccsp-source-seniors-safely-at-home). Younger adults with a physical disability or brain injury may be better served by [ICWP](/programs/icwp), adults with intellectual or developmental disabilities by the [NOW and COMP waivers](/programs/now-comp), and medically fragile children by [GAPP](/programs/gapp).

### Where to start

The front door for EDWP is your local **Area Agency on Aging**. Call Georgia's **Aging and Disability Resource Connection (ADRC) at 1-866-552-4464** to be connected to your region. They can explain the screening and assessment process, and they also know about local caregiver programs, support groups, and dementia resources. The Alzheimer's Association also runs a free **24/7 Helpline at 1-800-272-3900** for questions any time of day or night.

A practical tip: start the conversation early. Assessments and waiver enrollment can take time, and it is far easier to plan before a fall or hospital stay forces a rushed decision.

## Taking Care of the Caregiver

Dementia caregiving is a marathon, and the people who finish it are the ones who accept help along the way. Protecting your own health is not selfish. It is part of caring for your parent.

- **Ask for specific help.** "Can you sit with Dad on Tuesday afternoons?" gets a better response than "Let me know if you can help."
- **Use respite before you need it.** Regular breaks prevent crises; waiting until you are exhausted makes recovery harder.
- **Find your people.** Support groups, in person or online, connect you with others who understand exactly what you are living through.
