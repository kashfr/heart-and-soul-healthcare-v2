Most families do not choose a home health provider on a calm afternoon with time to research. They choose one from a hospital room, with a discharge planner handing over a printed list and a nurse saying the words "by Friday." It is one of the most consequential decisions a family makes, and it is almost always made under pressure.

This guide is meant to make that decision easier. It walks through how home health is regulated in Georgia, how to verify that an agency is legitimate, and the questions that actually separate a good provider from a mediocre one.

## First, Know Which Kind of Provider You Need

In Georgia, "home health" and "home care" are two different things with two different licenses. Getting this distinction right early will save you a great deal of time.

**Home Health Agencies** provide skilled, medical care ordered by a physician: skilled nursing, wound care, medication management, physical and occupational therapy. This care is typically covered by Medicare when someone is homebound and needs intermittent skilled care, and by Medicaid through Georgia's waiver programs.

**Private Home Care Providers (PHCP)** provide non-medical support: help with bathing, dressing, grooming, toileting, meal preparation, light housekeeping, and companionship. Families often pay for this privately, or cover it through a Medicaid waiver.

> Many families need both, and many agencies hold both licenses. The question to ask is not "are you a home health agency?" but "are you licensed to provide the specific services my loved one needs?"

Both provider types are licensed by the **Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH)** through its Healthcare Facility Regulation division. Home health agencies face the higher bar: in addition to licensure, they generally need a Certificate of Need, Medicare certification, and a registered nurse serving as director of services.

## Verify the License Before Anything Else

This is the single most important step, and it takes about two minutes. Georgia DCH publishes a public [license verification tool](https://forms.dch.georgia.gov/hfrd/Verify_License.html) where you can search any facility by name or address and confirm its licensure status and effective date.

Before you sign anything, confirm:

- The agency appears in the DCH database under the **exact legal name** on your paperwork
- The license is **current**, not expired or pending
- The **license type matches the care you need** (home health versus private home care)
- The address on file matches the office you have been dealing with

If an agency cannot be found, or the name on the contract differs from the name on the license, stop and ask why. Sometimes there is a legitimate explanation, such as a parent company or a recent name change. Sometimes there is not.

## Confirm They Accept Your Coverage

An excellent agency that does not bill your program is not an option. Ask directly, and ask specifically.

Georgia's home- and community-based programs each have their own approved provider networks:

- **[The Elderly and Disabled Waiver Program (EDWP)](/programs/edwp)**, delivered through CCSP and SOURCE, for older adults who would otherwise need nursing-facility care
- **[The Independent Care Waiver Program (ICWP)](/programs/icwp)**, for adults with physical disabilities or traumatic brain injury
- **[The NOW and COMP waivers](/programs/now-comp)**, for individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities
- **[The Georgia Pediatric Program (GAPP)](/programs/gapp)**, for medically fragile children who need in-home skilled nursing

Ask whether the agency is an enrolled provider for your specific program, not just "whether they take Medicaid." Those are different questions, and the second one gets a yes far more often than it should.

## Check the Public Quality Data

For Medicare-certified home health agencies, CMS publishes quality data on **Medicare Care Compare** at [medicare.gov/care-compare](https://www.medicare.gov/care-compare/). Agencies receive two separate star ratings:

- **Quality of Patient Care**, calculated from clinical measures such as improvement in mobility and bathing, timely initiation of care, and hospitalization risk
- **Patient Survey (HHCAHPS)**, which reflects communication, professionalism, and whether families would recommend the agency

Treat these as a starting point rather than a verdict. Ratings are only displayed for agencies that submit at least 40 completed patient surveys in the reporting period, so a strong small agency may have no rating at all. A missing rating is not a red flag. A consistently low one is worth asking about.

## The Questions That Actually Reveal Quality

Every agency will tell you they provide compassionate, high-quality care. These questions get past the brochure.

### On staffing and consistency

- **Will my loved one see the same caregiver each visit?** Continuity matters enormously, especially for someone with dementia or complex needs. Ask what their consistency rate actually is.
- **Who covers when the regular caregiver is sick or on vacation?** An agency without a real answer here will leave you with an empty schedule at the worst possible moment.
- **How do you screen and train staff?** Ask about background checks, competency verification, and ongoing training.
- **What is your caregiver turnover rate?** High turnover is the most reliable predictor of an inconsistent experience.

### On communication and escalation

- **Who do I call at 9pm on a Saturday?** Get a specific answer, not a general line.
- **How will I know what happened during a visit?** Ask whether you will receive visit notes and how quickly.
- **What happens if a caregiver does not show up?** Ask about their process, and their track record.
- **How do you handle a change in condition?** Ask who contacts the physician, and how fast.

### On the plan of care

- **Who develops the care plan, and how often is it reviewed?** For skilled care this should involve a registered nurse.
- **How is the plan adjusted if needs change?** Needs almost always change.
- **Can we meet the caregiver before service begins?** A good agency will say yes.

## Watch for These Warning Signs

Most agencies in Georgia are legitimate and well-run. A few are not. Be cautious when you encounter:

- Pressure to sign immediately, or discouragement from comparing other providers
- Reluctance to provide a license number or written service agreement
- Vague answers about cost, or about what your program covers
- A request for a large payment up front, especially in cash
- Guarantees about Medicaid approval, which no provider can honestly make
- No clear supervisory structure, or no named registered nurse for skilled care

> Trust your instincts during the first conversation. The way an agency treats you while it is trying to earn your business is usually the best available preview of how it will treat you once it has.

## Take the Site Visit Seriously

![An adult daughter and her father sit across a desk from a home health agency's director of nursing in a bright professional office, asking questions while she answers openly, a folder of documents on the desk between them, diverse Georgia community, plain neutral office walls, natural daylight from a window, photorealism, professional editorial photography, warm tones, 3:2 aspect ratio](/images/blog/how-to-choose-home-health-provider-georgia-site-visit.png)

If you can, visit the office and ask to speak with the director of nursing or clinical supervisor, not only an intake coordinator. Ask how long the leadership team has been in place and whether they can share references from families with similar needs. You are not being difficult by asking. You are doing exactly what a careful family should do.

## Making the Decision

When you have narrowed the field, weigh three things together: **license and coverage** (can they legally and financially serve you), **capability** (can they deliver the specific care your loved one needs), and **fit** (do you trust the people you spoke with).

The first two are verifiable. The third is a judgment call, and it matters more than families expect. You will be speaking with this agency often, sometimes on hard days. Choose people you feel comfortable calling. And if circumstances change, you are not locked in. Families switch providers, and a good agency will support a smooth transition rather than obstruct it.
